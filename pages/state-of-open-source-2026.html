The State of Open Source in 2026

Funding models are shifting, maintainer burnout is still the industry's quiet crisis, and a handful of foundations are trying something new. A field report.

Daniel Reyes
Senior Infrastructure Engineer

Open source software runs an enormous share of the internet's infrastructure, and the people maintaining the most critical parts of it are, in a lot of cases, still doing it unpaid, in their spare time, under increasing pressure from companies that depend on their work without contributing back. That tension isn't new, but 2026 has brought a few real shifts worth tracking.

Corporate funding is getting more direct, and more targeted

For years, corporate support for open source mostly meant sponsoring foundations and hoping the money trickled down to the projects that needed it. That's changing. More companies are now funding specific maintainers or specific projects directly, often through platforms built for exactly that purpose, rather than routing everything through a broad foundation budget.

This is a mixed improvement. Direct funding gets money to the people actually doing the work faster and more reliably. It also means funding decisions increasingly reflect which projects have the loudest corporate users, not which projects are most critical or most under-resourced — a security-critical parsing library used by everyone but championed by no single company still struggles to get attention under this model.

The projects most likely to break the internet if they fail are often the least likely to have a company publicly attached to funding them.

Maintainer burnout hasn't gone away — it's become measurable

What's new isn't the burnout itself, which maintainers have been describing for years, but that several major foundations have started actually surveying and publishing data on it, rather than treating it as an anecdotal problem. The resulting numbers are grim in a way that's hard to argue with once they're written down: a large share of maintainers of widely-used projects report considering stepping back within the next year, citing unpaid support burden and unrealistic user expectations far more often than the technical work itself.

What's actually being tried

  • Paid triage roles funded by foundations, separate from the maintainer doing the core development work
  • Mandatory response-time expectations removed from project READMEs, replaced with explicit statements about volunteer capacity
  • A small but growing number of companies formally allocating engineer time to upstream maintenance as part of the job, not as a side project

Licensing fights are back, and they're not going away quietly

Several previously permissively-licensed projects have shifted toward more restrictive licenses aimed specifically at large cloud providers who repackage open source software as a paid service without meaningfully contributing back. The pattern is now familiar enough to have a name in industry conversation, and it consistently produces the same fork: a community version keeps the original license, a commercial fork appears from the affected cloud vendor, and users are left choosing sides based on which trade-offs matter more to them.

There's no consensus on whether this protects maintainers or fragments ecosystems — reasonable people in the community disagree, often heatedly, and the disagreement itself has become a recurring story rather than a settled question.

What foundations are trying next

A handful of foundations have begun experimenting with something closer to a subscription model for enterprise users of critical infrastructure projects — not a license fee, but a support and priority-response tier, with the resulting revenue explicitly earmarked for maintainer compensation rather than foundation overhead. It's early, and it's a small fraction of overall project funding so far, but it's one of the few models on the table that doesn't rely on a single company deciding to be generous.

The honest summary

Nothing about 2026 has solved the underlying problem — critical infrastructure maintained by underpaid volunteers under corporate pressure — but for the first time in a while, there's real experimentation happening rather than just complaint. Whether any of these models actually stick will depend less on their design and more on whether the companies that benefit most from open source decide, this time, to pay for what they use before the projects they depend on quietly disappear.
