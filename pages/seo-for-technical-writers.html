SEO Fundamentals Every Technical Writer Should Know

You don't need to become a marketer. You need eight ideas that quietly change how discoverable your writing is — this is the short version.

Maya Okonkwo
Content Strategist & Editor

Technical writers tend to have a healthy allergy to marketing advice, and for good reason — a lot of SEO content is written to rank for "SEO," not to actually help anyone. This is the version aimed at people who just want their genuinely useful writing to reach the people searching for it.

Write the title a person would actually type

The single highest-leverage thing you can do is make your title match the phrase someone would realistically search. "Thoughts on State Management" is a fine title for a talk; it's a poor title for a post, because nobody searches that. "React State Management: When to Use Context vs. Redux" matches an actual question people type into a search bar, and it tells a search engine — and a human scanning results — exactly what they'll get.

Structure your headings like an outline, not decoration

Search engines use your heading hierarchy to understand what a page is actually about, section by section. That means one H1 per page (your title), H2s for main sections, and H3s nested under the relevant H2 — not skipped levels for visual reasons, and not headings chosen because they sound clever rather than because they describe the section.

A heading's job is to tell a skimming reader — human or machine — exactly what they'll find if they keep reading. Cleverness is a poor substitute for clarity here.

Answer the question in the first two sentences

A frustrating amount of technical content buries the actual answer under three paragraphs of preamble. Search engines increasingly reward — and readers definitely reward — content that states the direct answer near the top, then goes on to explain the reasoning and nuance for anyone who wants it. This isn't about dumbing content down; it's about respecting that some readers need the quick answer and others need the full explanation, and both should be able to get what they came for.

Internal links are doing more work than you think

Every time you link from one of your own posts to another, you're doing two things at once: helping a reader find related context, and telling search engines that the linked page is relevant to the topic at hand. Technical writers who write a lot of related content and never link between pieces are leaving an easy win on the table — go back through your older posts periodically and add links to newer, related ones.

  • Link using descriptive text, not "click here" — the link text itself is a relevance signal
  • Link to your own related posts from new content, not just the reverse
  • Don't force links where they don't genuinely help the reader — irrelevant links hurt more than no link

Alt text is accessibility first, SEO second — and that order matters

Write image alt text to describe what the image actually shows, for someone using a screen reader. Done well, this also happens to be exactly what search engines want, because it's genuinely descriptive rather than keyword-stuffed. "Screenshot of the CSS container query syntax" beats "CSS container queries SEO responsive design" on both counts.

Meta descriptions are ad copy for a result nobody paid for

The meta description doesn't affect ranking directly, but it's often the deciding factor in whether someone clicks your result over the one next to it. Write one to two sentences that describe what the reader will actually get, using natural language rather than a keyword list — think of it as the one sentence you'd say out loud if someone asked "what's this article about?"

Update old posts instead of only publishing new ones

A well-ranking older post that gets refreshed with current information tends to hold or improve its position; the same post left untouched for years tends to slowly decay as newer, more current content overtakes it. A quarterly pass through your most-visited older posts — fixing outdated details, adding a new section, updating the publish date when the update is substantial — is often more valuable than writing something new.
